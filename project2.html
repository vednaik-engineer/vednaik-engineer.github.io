MIT 2.007 – Design and Manufacturing I

Autonomous Pit-Stop Robot

I designed and built two robots for 2.007's "F007: Design to Survive" competition, an F1-themed head-to-head contest, including a pit-stop robot that was the only design in the class to complete a full tire change fully autonomously.

Class 2.007
Year Spring 2026
Result Top 4, DQ in quarterfinals

Two robots for 2.007's F1-themed "F007: Design to Survive" competition: an autonomous pit-stop robot and a human-driven racer. The pit-stop robot was the only design in the class to complete a full tire change autonomously, and both entries were competitive enough for a top-4 seed out of nearly 100 competitors, before a quarterfinal disqualification over a rules dispute.

The autonomous pit-stop robot completing a tire change

Strategy

Scoring rewarded repetition over a single clean run, so I planned to cycle the tire-change task as many times as possible in the two-minute match, with a secondary "mini-me" robot optionally sent out for bonus laps.

Requirements
  • ≥55 N of sustained vertical lift force
  • Non-backdrivable lift mechanism
  • Nut removal in under 10 seconds
  • Reliable self-alignment to the tire/nut
Nut Driver

I originally geared it 2:1 for torque margin that turned out unnecessary, costing half its speed, so I redesigned it to drive the socket directly, with added shaft support to carry the nut's mass in the socket.

Lift Mechanism

A servo drives an ACME lead screw into a wedge pair, converting rotation into lift. The tire needs only a few millimeters of travel to clear the hub, so the full stroke is just 0.65 in, guided by an integrated slot that keeps the lifting block from sliding as it's pushed up.

Lift Force

Combined mechanical advantage of the screw and wedge: roughly 1,359 N available against the 55 N requirement, intentionally overbuilt against friction and misalignment.

Drivetrain

Two LDO 217:1 motors drive the wheels directly, chosen for torque and traction over top speed. At stall (≈2.1 Nm), each wheel delivers roughly 61 N of horizontal force.

Frame

I built the frame around minimizing part count, cutting slotted joints and alignment features directly into it along with integrated guides for the lift mechanism. I placed the front axle as far forward as possible to center the loaded mass between the axles.

Lessons Learned
  • Compound-angle milling takes far longer than flat features
  • Shaft misalignment compounds fast
  • Excess factor of safety costs speed and mass
